Research

### 1. Digital Capital and Corporate Growth
My research examines how firms' data assets and artificial intelligence (AI) capital shape corporate investment and growth. By developing firm-level measures of digital capital, I investigate how these intangible resources influence both organic investment and acquisitions, with particular attention to firm size, investment efficiency, and strategic complementarities. This research seeks to understand how emerging digital capabilities reshape firms' growth strategies and resource allocation.


[Artificial Intelligence Capital and Complementary Acquisitions](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1eEJXtEjiHfMUT3xGgAdIjF1kTW5GfF1e/view?usp=drive_link){:target="_blank" } (Solo-authored). **Job Market Paper**.
- Main findings: This paper develops a firm-level measure of AI capital stock by combining AI-skilled employment data with a capitalization model that separates the accumulated quantity of AI capital from its shadow price. Greater AI capital predicts a higher likelihood of acquisition and stronger subsequent demand for AI-skilled labor, beyond conventional measures of AI employment and other intangible capital. AI-rich acquirers are also more likely to select targets with AI-skilled employees, consistent with complementarity between accumulated AI capabilities and externally acquired human capital.
- Presentations: NTU Academy of AI for Business and Social Science Research Poster 2026, International Finance and Banking Society Annual Conference (IFABS Ningbo) 2026

### 2. Information, Learning, and M&A
This research explores how information quality, information production, and learning shape acquisition decisions and outcomes. I examine how noise in market prices influences takeover valuations and how information generated through previous acquisitions affects subsequent deal performance. Together, these studies investigate how the availability, quality, and production of information influence decision-making and value creation in the market for corporate control.

[Share Price Noise and the 52-Week High Effect in M&A](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1i0QTdoGMnttnxHFrSVfVea26Ls04gZNa/view?usp=drive_link){:target="_blank" } (with Leonidas G. Barbopoulos, Yue Liu, and Jo Danbolt). _Working Paper_.
- Main findings: This paper examines how the informational composition of target stock prices shapes reference-point dependence in takeover pricing and outcomes. A higher share of price noise strengthens the sensitivity of takeover premiums to the target's 52-week high, but the associated bidder announcement-return penalty disappears, while the completion advantage of offers above the 52-week high weakens. The amplification effect is concentrated among undervalued targets, suggesting that stronger reference-point dependence in noisier information environments does not necessarily imply bidder overpayment.
- Presentations: AFA2025-Poster, EFMA2025, FMA2025

[Learning Without Owning: Absorptive Capacity and Corporate Acquisitions](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1IL041XKe8ivPDwu4g2SF3r2aYo2GxLZR/view?usp=drive_link){:target="_blank" } (Solo-authored). _Working Paper_.
- Main findings: This paper examines how firms' absorptive capacity shapes acquisition activity and target selection. It documents two contrasting patterns. Firms with greater absorptive capacity are more likely to undertake acquisitions. Yet, conditional on acquiring, they exhibit a lower relative preference for patent-rich targets. High-absorption acquirers also exhibit a stronger preference for targets with better operating performance and greater product-market similarity. The results suggest that absorptive capacity raises the benefits of integrating acquired assets while reducing the incremental knowledge benefits of owning patent-rich firms, thereby shaping both acquisition activity and corporate ownership decisions.

### 3. Financing and Contracting in M&A
My research investigates how corporate financing arrangements and acquisition contracts influence merger activity and post-acquisition outcomes. I study how pre-existing convertible debt affects firms' acquisition decisions and how contingent payment provisions, such as earnouts, shape post-merger operating efficiency. This research highlights the role of financial flexibility and contractual design in facilitating acquisitions and influencing their real economic outcomes.

Convertible Debt and Merger Activities (with Leonidas G. Barbopoulos and Ioannis Paraskevopoulos). _Work in Progress_.
- Main findings: This paper examines how firms' pre-existing convertible debt influences acquisition activity and outcomes. Firms with greater reliance on convertible debt are more likely to become acquirers, whereas conventional leverage exhibits the opposite relation. These findings highlight the distinct role of convertible debt in corporate financing and suggest that the composition of debt financing, rather than leverage alone, matters for firms' ability to pursue inorganic growth.

The Efficiency Effect of Earnout Contracts in M&A (with Leonidas G. Barbopoulos and Peter Cincinelli). _Work in Progress_.
- Main findings: This paper examines how contingent payment contracts influence post-acquisition operating efficiency. Acquisitions involving earnout provisions are associated with greater improvements in acquirer operating efficiency, particularly among smaller acquirers. These efficiency gains are not fully reflected in announcement-period stock returns, suggesting that acquisition contract design provides additional information about merger performance and can shape real post-acquisition outcomes.
